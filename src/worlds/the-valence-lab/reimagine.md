# The Valence Lab, reimagined — "Hands"

Claude's rethink, 2026-09-01, on James's brief: take a huge step back, roll the
dice ten times, and say what you would build if none of this existed and the
challenge were "engage high-school kids with how atoms work, what they look
like, how valence shells work, and how atoms snap together because of their
electrons. Fun, engaging, maybe a game structure, but don't turn it into a
game just because I said so."

The full version of this document is a published page with a live feel sketch
(drag atoms, watch hands pair, sodium hand its electron to chlorine, helium
bounce everything off) and a glyph per roll:

Local copy: `tmp/the-valence-lab/reimagine/valence-reimagined.html`
(gitignored; serve it through the dev server on 4174). This file is the text
and the prompt, so they survive.

Status: a proposal. Nothing in the world folder changed. Awaiting James's calls
(section 8) and his go on the first gate (section 7).

## 1. The diagnosis

1. It's an instrument, not a toy. Vials, a scope, six panels of prose and
   sliders. You operate it. Nobody has ever played it.
2. The truth is the default, so there's nothing to reveal. The real
   Hartree-Fock density is the most impressive thing we own and it's on screen
   before anyone has earned it, reading as a blob. Truth works as a reward. It
   fails as wallpaper.
3. Reading comes before doing. The panels explain valence; nothing lets you
   feel one. The bond is instant and invisible, a state change with a flash.

## 2. Ten rolls of the dice

Each starts from zero. What a kid walks away knowing; how it dies.

1. **Snap** (the pick). Atoms are luminous magnets. Every unpaired outer
   electron is a hand reaching out; drag two atoms together and the hands grab,
   click, and lock. Full shells bounce everything off. No menus, no reading,
   just fidgeting until molecules happen. Learns: valence is a count of hands;
   a full shell wants nothing. Dies if the physics feels floaty, or the *why*
   never surfaces.
2. **The Party.** Atoms as characters, personalities set by their valence.
   Sodium can't wait to give that one electron away. Chlorine is one seat short
   and will take it from anyone. Carbon has four hands and knows everybody.
   Helium won't talk to you. You host, you introduce, you watch. Learns:
   reactivity as motive. Dies if it gets cute and the personality replaces the
   physics.
3. **The Planetarium.** Fly inside one atom at the scale of a solar system,
   Orb Dimension style. Shells are nebulae in their true shapes, s spheres and
   p dumbbells. Watch them fill as a light show. Bring a second atom in and see
   two clouds merge into a bond, from orbital data we already compute and throw
   away. Learns: what an atom actually looks like. Dies if it's all wonder and
   no hands.
4. **Octet.** The outer shell as a ring of eight seats. Electrons are beads
   that want to sit in pairs. A bond is a seat shared by two rings. Puzzle
   levels: seat everyone. Lewis dot diagrams made physical and alive. Learns:
   the octet rule cold, plus bond orders and lone pairs. Dies if it becomes a
   worksheet with lighting.
5. **The Foundry.** A forge. Elements are glowing ingots on the wall; drop
   them on the anvil, squeeze, and the electrons redistribute in sparks. The
   finished molecule rises into a display case as its real electron cloud, the
   trophy. Recipes unlock. Learns: molecules are built, and there's a true
   shape at the end. Dies if it's a menu with a fire texture.
6. **Element Island.** The periodic table as terrain. Groups are ridges,
   periods are valleys, electronegativity is altitude, atomic radius is how
   wide each tile is. Walk it. Stand on chlorine and its rings assemble around
   you. Learns: the table's shape is the point; trends as geography. Dies if
   walking is the game and chemistry is scenery.
7. **Powers of Ten.** Start at a glass of water. Zoom, continuously, until the
   molecule appears, then the atom, then the cloud. At the bottom, pull the
   molecule apart and see why it held. Learns: this stuff is real and it's in
   your hand. Dies if it's a video: one journey, one thing.
   Spun off 2026-09-01 as its own world draft, "Powers of Ten (it ends
   somewhere silly)", in the admin panel's page drafts: James wants every zoom
   to bottom out somewhere ridiculous (a Wendy's, below the Planck length is
   the mall, a clown face, a cookie, a big turtle, another Elastic Space
   world).
8. **The Docking Bay.** Atoms are ships; open valence is a docking port; a
   bond is a clamp. Fly hydrogen into oxygen's port. Ionic bonding is one ship
   handing over a fuel cell and then clinging by charge. Learns: bonds have
   geometry; ports sit at real angles. Dies if the ship hides the electron.
9. **Chords.** Every element is a note. A molecule is a chord. A full shell is
   a resolved cadence; a noble gas is a held tonic; an electron handoff is a
   glissando. You build molecules by ear as much as by eye. Learns: stability
   as consonance, remembered by sound. Dies if it's a synth with atoms painted
   on.
10. **The Bouncer.** Each atom is a club. The nucleus is the bouncer, the
    count on his sign (+11). Electrons are patrons; the octet is the velvet
    rope. Sodium's club has one patron loitering outside who'd rather be in
    chlorine's, which has one spot left. Learns: charge balance, and why ions
    happen. Dies if metaphor overload.

## 3. What I'd build

Snap, with Octet's anatomy, Chords' sound, and the Foundry's display case.

One screen. A dark arena. A shelf of the first eighteen elements pinned to one
edge. Pull an atom in and it comes alive: a hot core stamped with its proton
count, calm inner rings, and an outer ring where the electrons live. Paired
electrons sit snug in twos. Unpaired ones are hands, tendrils of light that
wave and reach, longer the hungrier the atom. Bring two open hands together and
they snap.

Working name: **Hands**. The Valence Lab is still a good name and it's James's;
the rename is a call, not a recommendation.

### The atom in five states

1. Open hands: oxygen, two hands, two lone pairs.
2. Paired: water, both hands shaken.
3. Complete: neon, eight seats, all taken. Glows warm white, breathes.
4. Gave one away: sodium ion, outer ring gone, "+" badge.
5. Took one: chloride, the seat filled, "−" badge.

### The five moments

1. **The snap.** Hands meet, beads pair, and the pair sits in both rings at
   once as a lens of shared light. Click, flash, chord, lock. Double bonds
   share two pairs, triples three; the lens thickens and brightens.
2. **The grip.** Pull a molecule apart. A single bond stretches and pops. A
   double resists. A triple rubber-bands back. Bond strength as something you
   feel in your wrist, not a number in a panel.
3. **The handoff.** Sodium's one outer electron is far out and dim, barely
   held. Near chlorine's empty seat it leaves: slides across, sodium's ring
   collapses and glows complete with a plus, chlorine's fills with a minus.
   They cling by charge with a tether, not a lens. Two kinds of bond, shown by
   what the electron did.
4. **The refusal.** Throw anything at helium: a thud, one ripple through the
   full ring, nothing sticks. Methane with all four hands used bounces a fifth
   hydrogen the same way. Refusals are the funniest part. They're also the
   lesson.
5. **The X-ray.** Press and hold a finished molecule and the cartoon fades into
   the real electron density (the Hartree-Fock cloud we already bake) with one
   line: *this is what it actually looks like.* Release and the toy comes back.
   The truth becomes the reward instead of the default. The orbital viewer
   (Phase B.5 in the world CLAUDE.md) plugs in here later: the hands as they
   really are.

### The layers around it

1. **Goal layer.** Ten face-down recipe cards along one edge: the nine we
   already bake, plus salt for the ionic story. Make one and it flips; the
   molecule joins a trophy shelf, spinning as its real cloud. No timer, no
   lives, no score. Free play is the default; the cards are the pull. A timed
   arcade mode is a separate go, if ever.
2. **Text layer.** Whispers. One short line beside an event, then gone:
   *Chlorine is one seat short. It will take that electron from anyone.* Hold on
   any atom for a three-line card. That is all the reading on the main screen.
   `content.js` gets mined for lines; the six panels do not survive.
3. **Sound.** Every element a pitch, by period. A bond is a chord, a full shell
   is a cadence, a bounce is a thud, the handoff is a glissando. Web Audio
   through the shared sound control. James voices the chords; the sketch's are
   placeholders.
4. **What stays honest.** The intro says once that beads and hands are a
   cartoon of the Lewis model. The X-ray is the measurement. O₂ shows its two
   unpaired electrons on the ring, as the current engine already insists.
   Nothing in `orbitals.js`, `density.js` or the solver changes, and the sims
   keep running.
5. **Geometry.** Pairs on a ring repel, so the ring rearranges: water bends
   because its two lone pairs push, methane spreads, carbon dioxide goes
   straight. In 3D that is the real shape. The sketch does it flat, which is
   why its methane is a square.
6. **Motion.** Atoms are alive: hands wave, full shells breathe, molecules
   tumble when flicked. The camera is calm: slow drift only, never a snap or a
   shake. Same restraint the current lab already keeps.

## 5. The feel sketch (what the page proves)

2D canvas, cartoon physics, nine elements (H He C N O F Ne Na Cl), Lewis-model
electrons. Drag atoms together and hands pair (bond order = hands available,
capped at three); yank to break (single gives, double resists, triple nearly
never); sodium hands its electron to chlorine or fluorine and they tether by
charge; full shells bounce with a ripple and a thud; ten recipe cards light as
molecules complete; each element is a pitch and bonds play chords. Physics is
sub-stepped at 240 Hz with a stiff length spring and a soft angular spring
(domains spread evenly, VSEPR-style, so water bends and methane squares up in
2D). It is only about the snap; the look is the prompt's job.

## 7. The prompt for Claude Design (paste as-is)

```
Design the screens for "The Valence Lab", an interactive web toy inside Elastic Space (a collection of strange, beautiful browser worlds) that teaches high-schoolers how valence shells work by letting them grab atoms and snap them together. Desktop-first at 1920 by 1080. It will be built in WebGL (three.js). You are designing the look, the screen anatomy, and the key moments, not the code.

WHO IT IS FOR
A curious 15-year-old who has never taken chemistry, and anyone older who wanders in. Register: a toy, not an instrument. Museum-exhibit confidence, arcade-cabinet delight, zero worksheet energy.

THE ONE IDEA EVERYTHING SERVES
Every atom has hands. An atom's unpaired outer electrons are drawn as reaching tendrils of light ("hands"). Bring two atoms with open hands together and they snap: the hands find each other, the electrons pair, and the pair sits in both atoms' outer rings at once. That snap (sound, light, the lock) is the product.

WHAT THE SCREEN CONTAINS (one screen, no pages)
1. The arena: a dark, deep, fog-lit void filling the window. Nothing in it but the atoms you have pulled in. No bench, no vials, no instrument.
2. The shelf: the whole periodic table as a low strip along the bottom edge, real layout, lanthanides and actinides in a drawer. Tinted by appetite: warm amber for the ones that want to give an electron away (lithium, sodium), cool violet for the ones one seat short (fluorine, chlorine), electric cyan for the sharers in the middle (carbon, nitrogen, oxygen), quiet warm white for the full ones (helium, neon, argon), a muted steel for the metals in the middle of the table. The first eighteen are lit and spawn an atom when dragged up into the arena; the rest are present but dim until the build reaches them, so the shelf itself is the promise.
3. The atom, the core design object. Nucleus: a small hot core stamped with its proton count ("+8"). Inner shells: tight calm rings close to the core. Outer shell: a wider ring of seats, eight seats, or two for hydrogen and helium. Electrons are bright beads. Paired electrons sit snug in twos on the ring (lone pairs, calm). Unpaired electrons are hands: a bead with a tendril reaching outward, waving, longer the hungrier the atom (fluorine reaches far, carbon reaches modestly). A full shell glows warm white and breathes slowly. Design the atom's five states: open hands, paired, complete, positive ion, negative ion.
4. The snap: two hands meet, beads pair, a bright lens of shared light appears where the two rings overlap. Click, flash, chord, lock. Double bonds show two shared pairs, triple bonds three; the lens gets thicker and brighter. Design the frame before, the frame of, and the frame after.
5. The handoff (ionic): sodium's single outer electron is drawn far out and dim, barely held. Near chlorine's one empty seat it leaves: it slides across, sodium's outer ring collapses and glows complete with a "+" badge, chlorine's ring fills and glows complete with a "-" badge, and the two cling by charge with a soft tether instead of a lens.
6. The refusal: throw anything at helium and it bounces with a dull thud; the full ring ripples once. A finished molecule with no hands left (methane) bounces extra hydrogens the same way.
7. The X-ray: press and hold a finished molecule and the cartoon fades into the real electron density, a smooth measured cloud (we have the true data), with one caption: "This is what it actually looks like." Release and the toy comes back.
8. Whispers: the only text layer. One short line appears beside an event and fades ("Chlorine is one seat short. It will take that electron from anyone."). Also a hold-to-read card on any atom, three lines at most. No panels, no slider decks, no settings drawer on the main screen.
9. The recipe rail: cards in chapters along one edge (basics, salts, chains, rings, big molecules, polymers, life, your own). Make one and its card flips and the molecule joins the made column on the opposite edge as a small spinning trophy, its real density cloud. Anything in the made column drags back into the arena as a block, which is how chains, rings and big molecules get built without starting from atoms every time. No timer, no lives, no score. Free play is the default; the cards are the pull.
10. Sound is part of the design: every element is a pitch, a bond is a chord, a full shell is a resolved cadence, a bounce is a thud, the handoff is a glissando. Show where sound state lives (a single mute, nothing more).
11. Heat: one dial, nothing else. Cold settles everything; hot shakes the arena and the weakest bonds go first: a drop of water scatters, then single bonds pop, then the salt crystal comes apart, and nitrogen's triple bond is the last thing standing.
12. Chains and rings: carbon's four hands make chains and rings. A pour gesture fills leftover hands with hydrogen in one motion. Benzene's ring shows a halo of shared electrons in the X-ray.

VISUAL DIRECTION
Dark and luminous. The ground is a deep hue-biased near-black (indigo or ink-teal, not pure black). Electrons are the brightest thing on screen; everything else is quieter than they are. Color carries meaning (appetite and charge), never decoration. Type: one characterful display face for the few large words, one comfortable text face for whispers, both large and generous; numbers use tabular figures. Motion: the atoms are alive (hands wave, full shells breathe), the camera is calm, slow drift only, never snaps or shakes.

DO NOT
Draw a lab bench, vials, beakers, or a scope. Use hex grids, reticles, HUD brackets, or "sci-fi UI" chrome. Make gray panels with sliders. Use the periodic table's textbook colors as decoration. Put explanatory paragraphs on the main screen. Use emoji.

DELIVERABLES, all at 1920 by 1080
1. Arena, idle: three atoms in the arena with open hands, shelf and recipe rail visible.
2. The snap: the exact frame a water molecule completes (oxygen's two hands taking two hydrogens), with the whisper.
3. The handoff: sodium and chlorine mid-transfer.
4. The X-ray: a finished molecule held, cartoon half-faded into the density cloud.
5. The trophy shelf with four cards flipped.
6. The atom in its five states as a design-system sheet, with the naming.
7. The arena mid-heat: a drop scattering, a chain losing a hydrogen, the crystal still holding.
Before committing to any of these, a mood board of three directions for the atom rendering (glass, plasma, neon wire). We pick one, then you build the six frames in it.
```

## 8. James's calls

1. **The name.** Keep The Valence Lab, or rename it Hands, Octet, or Snap.
2. **Flat or deep.** A 3D scene with drag in the view plane and a flick to
   tumble a molecule (Claude's recommendation), or a flat 2D toy like the
   sketch. 3D buys the real shapes; 2D ships faster and reads instantly.
3. **The rail.** Recipe cards visible from the start, or hidden until the
   first molecule forms.
4. **The shelf.** All eighteen elements, or the well-behaved dozen. Boron,
   beryllium, aluminium and the like have messy octet stories that a toy has to
   either fudge or explain.
5. **The voice.** Whispers deadpan, like the current lab's refusal copy, or
   warmer.
6. **Ionic in round one.** Claude's recommendation is yes: salt is the other
   half of the lesson, and the handoff is the best moment in the sketch.
7. **The harness first.** Build the hands lab in tmp and judge it by eye
   before any world code, the way the nebulae and the crust went, or go
   straight into the world.

## 9. Snap, fully grown (2026-09-01, later the same night)

James, after the page: "Nice presentation... you chose the best." Then his real
question: what does the advanced version look like when it loads, what are the
features, what happens with many more elements and chains of molecules, how
does it stay fun. This section is that answer. He also fixed one word: "hands"
is only the name for an unpaired electron's reach; the visual stays the tendril
of light from the sketch, nothing anthropomorphic, and the working name is
Snap, not Hands.

**When it loads.** Full-window dark arena, no chrome. Three atoms already
breathing (oxygen and two hydrogens, far apart) and one line: "Everything is
made of these." The whole periodic table as a low glowing strip along the
bottom, the first eighteen lit, the rest dim but present. A thin column of
recipe cards on the right, a thin column of things you've made on the left, a
heat dial and a mute in the corner. A return visit loads your arena as you
left it, with your collection and your lit-up table.

**The anatomy.**
1. The arena: fog-lit void, wheel to zoom, drag an atom to move it inside its
   molecule, drag a bond's lens to move the whole molecule, flick to tumble.
   The camera drifts to keep your work framed and never snaps.
2. The shelf: the whole table, real layout, tinted by appetite. Drag a tile up
   to spawn one atom; hold to pour a stream; hover for a three-line card.
   Tiles you've used stay lit, so completing the table is the collector's goal
   without a score.
3. Recipes: chapters (basics, salts, chains, rings, big molecules, polymers,
   life, your own). Flip a card by making it. "Your own" fills itself.
4. Made: everything you've built, spinning as its real cloud. Drag one back in
   and it returns as a block. That is how big things get built.
5. Whisper: one line, then gone. Hold any atom for a card.
6. Heat and mute: the only two controls.
7. The lens: hold a molecule for the real density, hold a lone atom for the
   real orbitals. Never a mode, always a gesture.

**Scaling to all 118.** The hands cartoon covers the s and p blocks honestly;
the rest of the table needs three additions that are also features:
1. A third bond type. Sharing and giving are in the sketch. Metals pool: drop
   twenty coppers and they lattice, and their outer electrons become a sea
   that flows through the block.
2. Flexible hands. Transition metals show two or three possible hand counts
   (iron gives two or three, depending on who's asking); the badge on the
   nucleus changes as partners arrive.
3. Decay. Heavy elements tick, flash, and become something else; uranium spits
   an alpha particle that grabs two electrons and turns into helium. (In the
   vision, out of the mock, by agreement.)
4. Exceptions as easter eggs: xenon can be talked into bonding by fluorine;
   hydrogen bonding makes water drops; francium in water does what it does.
5. The f-block lives in a drawer under the strip.

**Chains and big molecules.**
1. Carbon does the work: link carbons, pour hydrogens over the skeleton to
   fill the open hands in one gesture, and you have octane. Close six into a
   ring and the X-ray shows the delocalized halo.
2. Blocks: anything on the made shelf drags back in as a unit; functional
   groups come as named blocks (OH, NH2, COOH).
3. Polymerize: a chain verb on ethylene; each unit opens its double bond and
   links to the next (the old Phase D, reborn as a gesture).
4. Big cards: glucose, caffeine (the first boss), aspirin, capsaicin,
   nicotine, chlorophyll as the final boss with a magnesium at the center.
5. Naming and honesty: known molecules get named; unknown ones get "we haven't
   measured that one" and a shimmer. The good version runs the Hartree-Fock
   solver we already wrote in a web worker for small unbaked molecules, so the
   X-ray of a thing nobody prepared computes on the spot and says so.

**Why it stays fun at scale.** The verbs stay few (drag, flick, pour, shake,
fill, chain, hold, pull, throw); new chemistry arrives as new results of the
same verbs. Emergence carries the long tail (crystals, drops, metal seas,
growing chains, decay). Chapters, not levels: nothing is locked, things are
dim until touched. Sound scales with the thing: caffeine is a cluster chord, a
polymer is a loop, a metal is a drone.

**The mock (built the same night on his go).** The page now has a section,
"Snap, fully grown": a 16:9 loaded screen on the sketch's engine. The whole
table on the shelf (118 tiles + the drawer; the first eighteen spawn, the rest
whisper "not in this build yet"), recipes in chapters with four cards already
flipped, a made column of four tiny live renders, and an arena that loads with
an octane chain, a benzene ring, a 4x4 salt crystal and a seven-water drop held
by hydrogen bonds. Drag anything; hold a molecule for the X-ray (benzene shows
its halo); the heat dial shakes the arena and pops the weakest bonds first
(measured: at full heat octane shatters in seconds while benzene's double bonds
and the crystal hold). Engine additions for it: per-scene center pull, a heat
term with a heat-only break rule, hydrogen-bond attraction between waters,
explicit bond orders for building rings, and a projected-tetrahedral slot
layout for four-domain atoms (110/90/70/90 degrees instead of a square, which
is what made the chain read as a chain). Nine more elements have data now
(Li Be B Mg Al Si P S Ar). Local copy: tmp/the-valence-lab/reimagine/
valence-reimagined.html (KEEP); dev-snapshot captures in tmp/snapshots/
snap-*.png.

**The Design handoff (agreed).** Order of operations: the mock here proves
the anatomy and the feel; Claude Design gets the prompt (section 6, updated to
the grown version) and returns the look; we code the look onto the working
engine rather than re-deriving the engine from a picture. Design cannot judge
motion, physics or sound, so nothing about the feel is delegated to it.
